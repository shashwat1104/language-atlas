Definition, scope and purpose — what the industry sells, who pays for it, how big it is, where it ends, and why its borders are moving.

1. A working definition

The language industry is the set of businesses, institutions and people that are paid to make meaning travel across languages, and to build the knowledge, tools and data that let them do it.

Three parts of that sentence matter:

  • "Paid to" — the industry is defined by a transaction. A bilingual nurse who helps a patient is using language skills; a hospital buying an interpreting service is the language industry.
  • "Make meaning travel across languages" — the core product is not words but understanding. A translation that is accurate word-for-word but misleads the reader has failed.
  • "Knowledge, tools and data" — teaching people languages, building translation software and producing language data for AI are part of the same economy, because they all exist to serve cross-language communication.
No single official definitionThere is no legal or statistical definition of "the language industry". The largest research firm, Nimdzi, publishes no formal definition and covers translation, localization, interpreting and related services[1]. Slator measures a narrower "language solutions and AI" market[6]; CSA Research measures "language services and technology"[3]. Government statistics usually hide the industry inside "professional services" or "other business services". This Atlas uses the wider, six-sector definition above and says which definition every number belongs to.

2. Why the industry exists

Organisations do not buy translation because they like languages. They buy it because something goes wrong — or cannot happen — without it. Five reasons cover almost all spending:

#ReasonWhat the buyer is really buyingTypical examplesPrice sensitivity
1RevenueAccess to customers in another marketApp store listings, product pages, game launches, ad campaignsHigh for volume content, low for launch-critical content
2CompliancePermission to operate: a regulator, court or law requires the languageDrug labels, device instructions, court filings, financial disclosuresLow — the cost of non-compliance is far higher
3SafetyPeople not getting hurt because they misunderstoodHospital interpreting, safety manuals, aviation, clinical trialsLow on paper, often under-funded in practice
4Rights and accessEqual access to public services, justice, education, mediaCommunity interpreting, captions, sign language, multilingual government portalsBudget-bound; driven by law and policy
5UnderstandingKnowing what others are saying or writingBusiness intelligence, research, diplomacy, M&A due diligence, eDiscoveryVery high — "gist" quality is often enough, so MT dominates

Each reason creates a different market. Revenue-driven content is where AI is changing prices fastest, because buyers can measure the trade-off between cost and conversions. Compliance and safety content is where human accountability is stickiest, because someone must sign for the result. Understanding-driven content was the first to move almost entirely to machines.

Insider view

Ask any buyer "what happens if this translation is wrong?" The answer — nothing, lost sales, a fine, a lawsuit, a death — predicts their workflow, their vendor, their price and how much AI they will accept far better than the content type does. Mature buyers formalise this as risk tiers, and it is the single most useful idea in modern localization.

Behind all five reasons sits a supply-and-demand gap that is enormous: the world produces content far faster than it translates it. CSA Research claims that less than 0.000004% of global content is translated today[3]. That figure is not independently checkable and should be read as illustrative rather than measured Low, but the direction is uncontested: most content is never translated, which is why cheaper translation tends to create new demand rather than only cannibalise old demand (see section 8).

3. The core vocabulary: GILT and its relatives

Practitioners describe the industry with four nested words, often shortened to GILT.

TermPlain meaningWho does itExampleIt is not…
Globalization (G11n)The business decision and operating model for serving many marketsExecutives, strategy, product leadershipDeciding to launch in Japan, Brazil and Germany next year, with local pricing and supportA language task. It is a company strategy.
Internationalization (i18n)Engineering a product so it can be adapted without rebuilding itSoftware engineers, designersStoring text outside the code; supporting UTF-8, right-to-left layouts, local date formats and plural rulesTranslation. No words change; the product becomes ready to change.
Localization (L10n)Adapting a product or content to one localeLocalization managers, LSPs, linguists, engineers, testersJapanese UI, yen pricing, Japanese payment methods, culturally adjusted imagery, tested on Japanese devicesOnly translation. Translation is usually the biggest part, but not the whole.
Translation (T9n)Moving written meaning from one language to anotherTranslators, editors, MT engines, LLMsThe Japanese text of each UI stringInterpreting (spoken or signed) or transcreation (rewriting for effect).

The numeronyms count the letters between the first and last: i-18 letters-n. Around these four sit related terms you will meet constantly:

  • Interpreting — real-time spoken or signed language. A separate profession with its own training, standards and economics, and a very large part of industry revenue (see section 6).
  • Transcreation — rewriting creative content so it lands the same way, even if the words change completely. Usually priced by the hour, not the word.
  • Locale — a language plus region, sometimes plus script: pt-BR, zh-Hant-TW, hi-IN. Localization targets locales, not languages. Spanish for Spain and Spanish for Mexico are two locales.
  • Language pair and direction — EN→JA and JA→EN are different products with different translator supply, rates and machine quality.
  • LSP / LSI / LTP — language service provider (the traditional "agency"); language solutions integrator (Slator's newer term for providers that combine people, technology and AI); language technology platform (software vendors). The renaming itself is a sign of how the industry sees AI[9].

Insider view

Large public bodies are both buyers and producers. The European Commission's translation directorate (DGT) describes itself as part of the language industry through its translation, editing and AI-based services[10] — and also sponsors the ELIS industry survey and runs its own machine translation. Any market estimate that only counts commercial revenue misses this in-house layer.

Why the numbers differ

  1. Scope. Does the number include interpreting? Technology? In-house teams? Language education? Data for AI? Each "yes" adds billions. Nimdzi's broad services view includes the very large US interpreting businesses; Slator's addressable view strips out what outside providers cannot sell into.
  2. Method. Nimdzi builds up from provider data; CSA models a defined population of about 27,000 providers from a sample[3]; generic vendors often work top-down from other reports.
  3. Double counting. When an MLV subcontracts to an SLV that pays a freelancer, the same euro shows up as revenue three times. Nimdzi explicitly warns about this[1].
  4. Deflation vs volume. AI is pushing price per word down while volume goes up. A market can shrink in dollars while translating more words than ever. Slator's 2025 figure fell 2.7%, with traditional LSIs down 5.1% and technology platforms up nearly 20%[7] — a shift of money from services to software, not necessarily a fall in demand.
  5. Currency and timing. Most providers report in EUR, GBP, JPY or CNY; conversion rates and fiscal years move the dollar totals.
Rule for using these numbersNever put a Nimdzi number next to a Slator number as if they were comparable. Compare each firm with its own past editions to see direction; quote ranges ("roughly USD 30–73bn depending on definition") when you need a single sentence; and always say what is measured.

Insider view

Read the direction the three specialists agree on, not the levels they disagree on: flat to slightly negative headline revenue in 2023–2025, technology and AI taking share from services, and growth expectations cut sharply — Nimdzi now projects less than 1% a year after 2026[1], Slator 2.65% a year to 2031[6]. That consensus is more reliable than any single total.

6. The shape of the industry

Extremely fragmented

No one owns this industry. In Nimdzi's figures the 100 largest providers together earned USD 14.3bn in 2025 — 19.8% of the market — and the top 10 alone took about half of that[1]. CSA's distribution tells the same story from a different base: top 10 at 8.1%, ranks 11–100 at 6.2%, and everyone else — thousands of small agencies and freelancers — at 85.7%[3].

Three lessons hide in this table:

  • Interpreting is huge. Three of the top six (LanguageLine, Sorenson, Propio) are interpreting businesses, and interpreting makes up 33.6% of top-100 revenue[1]. Writing that treats "the language industry" as "translation and localization" misses a third of the biggest players.
  • Verticals create giants. Keywords and Side are games companies; their scale comes from owning one vertical's full workflow (translation, voice, testing).
  • The industry is US- and Europe-headquartered but not only. Of the top 100, 42 are in Europe, 34 in North America, 17 in Asia and 7 in Oceania; none in South America or Africa[1].

A K-shaped market

Growth is uneven by size. In 2025, Nimdzi's top 10 grew 3.6% while providers ranked 51–100 shrank 4.3%; among top-100 providers, Asia-based firms grew 7.2% and North American 6.0% while European firms grew only 0.7%[1]. CSA describes the same split as a "K-shaped" market[3]. The middle — generalist, mid-sized agencies without a technology or vertical edge — is under the most pressure.

What providers actually sell

Among providers surveyed by Nimdzi, 94.6% offer translation and localization, 81.1% machine translation post-editing, 69.6% subtitling and 68.2% transcription; the most common generative-AI services are editing AI-generated content (72.0%) and AI-generated translation (70.3%)[1]. The most common verticals were financial and legal, technology, and life sciences; among the top 100, life sciences leads[1].

8. How AI is redrawing the definition

For 30 years the industry's unit of value was the translated word. That unit is breaking, and you can see it in four places.

8.1 The research firms renamed the market

Slator's flagship report described a "language services and technology industry" of USD 27.03bn in 2023[15]; its 2026 edition describes a "language solutions and AI" market of USD 30.85bn in 2025[6]. The vocabulary of "LSIs" and "LTPs" replaced "LSPs" and "tool vendors". (Verified fact: the renaming. Hypothesis: that it reflects how value is moving.)

8.2 Prices are being reset, not just cut

Nimdzi reports that some clients seek cost cuts of up to 75% through AI, that price pressure is providers' second-biggest challenge, and that more than three-quarters of providers plan to change at least some pricing models. In 2025, 56.5% of those changing introduced hourly rates instead of per-word, 50.0% fixed project prices, and 34.8% separate rates for human and pure-technology work[1]. In Europe, ELIS 2026 freelancer responses suggest agencies commonly impose post-editing discounts of around 29%[10] Medium.

8.3 Productivity per person is jumping

Nimdzi reports average revenue per employee among the top 100 of USD 414,931 in 2025, up 73.6% from about USD 239,000 in 2024, while overall staffing fell by less than 5%[1]. Treat the size of the jump with caution — it may partly reflect changes in who reported headcount — but the direction matches every other signal.

8.4 Ownership is reshuffling

  • In August 2025 TransPerfect, the largest provider, bought Unbabel, a venture-backed AI translation company, folding its TowerLLM model and COMET evaluation tools into its GlobalLink platform[12].
  • In August 2026 RWS agreed to acquire Acolad. Using 2025 figures, CSA reports Acolad revenue of about £182m against an enterprise value of about £22.4m — roughly 0.12× revenue — and argues that Acolad's debt position was decisive[11]. A large European LSP valued at about a tenth of a year's revenue is a striking signal of how investors now price traditional services.
  • Nimdzi counted 31 visible deals in 2025; only 27% of surveyed providers described themselves as active buyers, the lowest it has recorded, while 33% were open to selling[1].

8.5 Value is moving to data, workflow and accountability

Nimdzi found that proprietary AI and data services — data curation, prompt engineering, custom LLM integration — produced the largest revenue growth and best margin protection in 2025[1]. Slator sizes the data-for-AI market at about USD 9.3bn in 2026, growing about 18% a year[8]. The emerging picture (Atlas hypothesis, consistent with the sources): machines produce first drafts; the industry's value shifts to deciding what needs human attention, supplying the context and data that make machines good, and signing for the result where risk is high.

Shrink or expand?Does cheaper translation shrink the market or grow it? Both forces are real. Per-unit prices fall (deflation), but content that was never worth translating becomes worth translating (new volume — a Jevons-paradox effect). The specialist firms' flat dollar totals alongside rising volumes suggest that in 2023–2025 deflation and volume growth roughly cancelled out. Which one wins after 2026 is the central forecasting question of this Atlas (Volume 20).

9. The view from one corridor: India ↔ Japan

Definitions look different from inside a specific corridor. Applying this chapter to India–Japan work (Atlas observation; corridor data is covered properly in Vol 3.7):

  • Japan is a top-tier buyer and supplier. A Japanese company, Side, is in the global top 10[1], Japanese buyers sit inside Asia's USD 12.6bn of demand, and Asia-based top-100 providers grew fastest of any region in 2025 (+7.2%).
  • The corridor spans all six sectors. Japanese-language education and JLPT preparation (sector 3), JA↔EN and JA↔Indic translation and interpreting for automotive, IT and manufacturing (sector 1), recruitment-linked language assessment (sector 3), and Japanese and Indic speech data for AI (sector 4).
  • Direction matters. EN→JA localization for software and games is a large, mature market; JA→Hindi or JA→Tamil direct translation is thin and usually pivots through English — a quality and cost risk, and an opportunity.
  • Interpreting is under-served. On-site and remote Japanese interpreting for Japanese companies operating in India (plant start-ups, audits, negotiations) is a classic high-trust, hard-to-automate service.

10. What insiders know that outsiders do not

  1. The industry sells risk reduction, not words. Price follows the cost of being wrong.
  2. Interpreting is a third of the top end. Many "localization industry" maps leave it out.
  3. Most revenue passes through more than one company. Prime → regional vendor → freelancer. Every layer takes a margin, which is why the linguist sees a fraction of the client's price (Vol 13.11).
  4. Linguistic assets are the lock-in. Translation memories and termbases make repeat work cheaper and switching vendors painful. Who owns them is a contract question.
  5. "Quality" means "fit for purpose", agreed in advance. ISO 5060 (2024), for example, guides evaluation of human, post-edited and raw MT output using error types and penalty points[13]; nobody serious scores "quality" without a typology and a threshold.
  6. Files cause more failures than language. Broken placeholders, lost tags and wrong encodings ship bugs more often than mistranslations do.
  7. Market reports disagree by 2×; directions agree. Use levels carefully, directions confidently.
  8. The middle is squeezed. Large firms with technology and small specialists with deep domain skills are doing better than generalist mid-sized agencies.

Chapter review

1 · What I learned

  • The language industry is defined by paid cross-language communication and the tools, data and knowledge behind it.
  • Five reasons drive spending: revenue, compliance, safety, rights/access, understanding.
  • GILT: globalization (strategy), internationalization (engineering), localization (adaptation), translation (language).
  • Six sectors: services, technology, education, data & AI, content & media, research & public infrastructure.
  • Size estimates range from about USD 31bn to 73bn because they measure different things.
  • The industry is highly fragmented; interpreting is a third of top-100 revenue.
  • AI is resetting pricing, productivity, ownership and the unit of value.

2 · Important terminology

GILT · i18n · L10n · G11n · locale · language pair · directionality · LSP · LSI · LTP · MLV · SLV · MTPE · transcreation · interpreting · TM · termbase · addressable market · risk tier · linguistic assets — all in the glossary.

3 · Key companies

TransPerfect, LanguageLine Solutions, RWS, Keywords Studios, Sorenson, Propio, Lionbridge, Translate Plus, GienTech, Side; Acolad; Unbabel. Research firms: Nimdzi, CSA Research, Slator. Bodies: European Commission DGT, ELIS partners, ISO, Unicode Consortium.

4 · Key tools

CAT tools (Trados Studio, memoQ), TMS platforms (Phrase, Smartling, XTM, GlobalLink), MT engines (DeepL, Google, Microsoft), general LLMs, evaluation tools such as COMET. Each is introduced properly in Volume 7.

5 · Important files

XLIFF (bilingual exchange), TMX (translation memory), TBX (terminology), JSON/PO (software strings), SRT/TTML (subtitles), IDML (layout). Volume 8 covers each in depth.

6 · Industry insights

  • Risk, not content type, predicts workflow and price.
  • Dollar totals are flat while volumes rise: deflation.
  • Value is shifting from words to data, workflow and accountability.
  • Ownership is consolidating at low valuations for traditional services.

7 · Common misconceptions

  • "Localization means translation." It also covers formats, payments, imagery, legal and testing.
  • "It's a small niche." Even the narrowest specialist estimate is about USD 31bn.
  • "Machine translation already ended the industry." Headline revenue is roughly flat, not collapsing; it is being redistributed.
  • "The big players are translation agencies." Several are interpreting or games companies.
  • "All market numbers measure the same thing." They do not.

8 · Practical exercise

Pick one company you know (for example a Japanese automaker with an Indian plant, or a SaaS app). In 30 minutes:

  1. List every piece of its content that crosses a language boundary.
  2. Tag each with one of the five reasons (revenue, compliance, safety, rights, understanding).
  3. Mark which of the six sectors would supply it.
  4. Rate each item's risk (low / medium / high) and say whether raw AI, AI + human review, or human-first would suit it.

You have just drafted a localization strategy and a risk-tier model.

9 · Quiz

  1. Which GILT layer involves no change to words at all?
    AnswerInternationalization — it makes a product ready to be adapted.
  2. Why can Nimdzi say USD 72.6bn and Slator USD 30.85bn for the same year without either being wrong?
    AnswerThey measure different scopes: Nimdzi a broad services market including interpreting; Slator an addressable "language solutions & AI" market. Methods also differ.
  3. What share of top-100 revenue comes from interpreting, according to Nimdzi?
    Answer33.6%.
  4. Name the five reasons organisations buy language services.
    AnswerRevenue, compliance, safety, rights and access, understanding.
  5. What is the difference between EN→JA and JA→EN as markets?
    AnswerDifferent translator supply, rates, buyers and machine quality — direction defines a separate market.
  6. What does "K-shaped" mean for this industry?
    AnswerSome providers (largest, tech-led, specialist) grow while others (mid-sized generalists) shrink — e.g. top 10 +3.6% vs ranks 51–100 −4.3% in 2025.
  7. Give one reason market totals double-count.
    AnswerSubcontracting: the same job is booked as revenue by the prime, the regional vendor and the freelancer.
  8. Which new pricing model was most common among providers that changed pricing in 2025?
    AnswerHourly rates instead of per-word (56.5%), per Nimdzi.

  • How reliable is the 2025 jump in revenue per employee?
  • Which of Nimdzi's two optimistic 2030 figures (USD 86.2bn or 96.9bn) is intended?

AI Impact & Opportunity Brief

1 · Subject analysedThe language industry as a whole (all six sectors, weighted toward services and technology, where data exists)
2 · AI Disruption Score & confidence7/10 · High · Confidence: Medium
3 · Current commercial AI deploymentAlready commercially deployed at scale: MT and LLM translation, MTPE, AI-assisted QA, ASR captioning. Commercially emerging: AI dubbing, agentic workflows, quality-estimation routing, machine interpreting. Experimental: fully autonomous high-risk translation.
4 · Core tasks automated todayFirst-draft translation of most text in high-resource languages; transcription; draft subtitles; terminology extraction; many automated QA checks; file analysis and quoting.
5 · Core tasks requiring humans todaySign-off on regulated and high-risk content; interpreting in healthcare, courts and sensitive negotiations; transcreation; low-resource languages; client relationships, scoping and accountability.
6 · Quality parityNear parity for routine content in high-resource pairs; clearly below parity for creative, culturally loaded, low-resource and spoken high-stakes work. (Atlas assessment; detailed evidence in Vol 10 and 15.)
7 · Cost & pricing impactStrong downward pressure on per-word prices; clients seeking cuts of up to 75%; shift to hourly, fixed and separated human/technology pricing[1].
8 · Margin & unit economicsMargins protected where providers own technology, data services or a vertical; compressed for generalist resellers of human translation.
9 · Demand expansion vs contractionBoth: deflation per unit, expansion in volume. Dollar totals flat to slightly down 2023–2025 across specialist sources.
10 · Employment & rolesTotal provider staffing roughly stable in 2025 (down less than 5%, per Nimdzi); freelancer sentiment in Europe negative (ELIS 2026). Roles shift from translating to reviewing, configuring, evaluating and data work.
11 · Most vulnerableMid-sized generalist agencies; per-word-only business models; freelancers selling undifferentiated translation in high-resource pairs; venture-backed "AI translation" firms without distribution (cf. Unbabel's sale).
12 · Most resilientInterpreting (especially healthcare and legal); regulated life-sciences and legal specialists; technology platforms; providers with data-for-AI services; deep vertical owners (games, media).
13 · Adoption barriersLiability and regulation; data privacy and residency; quality in low-resource languages; buyer procurement habits; legacy file and tool chains.
14 · AcceleratorsFalling model costs; better multilingual LLMs; enterprise AI budgets; price pressure from procurement; agentic tooling inside TMS platforms.
15 · 2026–2027 outlookPricing-model shift becomes mainstream; consolidation at low multiples; AI-first workflows become default for low-risk content.
16 · 2028–2030 outlookStructural split: technology-led platforms and risk-accountable specialists on one side; shrinking pure-labour resellers on the other. Machine interpreting gains ground in low-stakes settings.
17 · 2031–2035+ outlookSpeculative: language transformation embedded inside most software; human language work concentrated in accountability, culture, live high-stakes speech, data and evaluation.
18 · AI Opportunity Score8/10 — very high for builders who target workflow, risk and data gaps rather than raw translation.
19 · Top 3 buildable ideas(1) A risk-tier classifier that routes content to raw AI, AI+review or human-first, with an audit trail. (2) An open-source "market-number checker" that tags any language-industry statistic with its source definition and comparability. (3) A pivot-language risk detector for indirect pairs (e.g. JA→EN→HI) that flags meaning drift.
20 · Strategic takeawayLearners: learn risk, files and workflow, not only languages. Builders: build where accountability and context are missing, not another translation box. Operators: price outcomes and risk, own your data, specialise.
